⚠ Critical Values Present
📈 Serum Phosphorus Rises before calcium — key early marker 9.1 mg/dL ↑ tap ▾
Rises first — earliest detectable abnormality.
The critical threshold is >8 mg/dL. In cholecalciferol toxicosis, phosphorus elevation precedes the calcium rise — this temporal sequence is diagnostically important and helps distinguish this toxicosis from other causes of hypercalcemia. Monitor phosphorus closely in early suspected cases even before calcium becomes overtly elevated.
🔴 Total Calcium Normal canine range: 9–11 mg/dL 15.2 mg/dL ↑↑ tap ▾
Critically elevated — above the 13 mg/dL diagnostic threshold.
A total calcium above 13 mg/dL confirms significant hypercalcemia. At 15.2 mg/dL, tissue mineralization is a real and immediate risk. Calculate the product: Ca × P = 15.2 × 9.1 = 138 mg/L — well above the critical threshold of 70 mg/L at which soft tissue mineralization occurs.
🔬 Parathyroid Hormone (PTH) Critical differentiating finding Low / Suppressed tap ▾
Suppressed PTH with high calcium — a key differentiator.
The parathyroid gland senses elevated serum calcium and responds by suppressing PTH. This is a normal feedback response — but in the context of toxicosis, it distinguishes cholecalciferol poisoning from primary hyperparathyroidism, where PTH would be inappropriately elevated despite high calcium. Low PTH + high calcium = toxicosis or malignancy, not primary hyperparathyroidism.
🫘 BUN & Creatinine Renal function markers Both Elevated tap ▾
Azotemia confirms acute kidney injury (AKI).
Calcium deposits in renal tubules directly impair glomerular filtration and tubular function. Elevated BUN and creatinine indicate that nephrotoxicity is already underway. Serial monitoring every 12–24 hours during hospitalization is essential. IV fluid diuresis is urgently needed to protect remaining renal function and promote calcium excretion.
💧 Urine Specific Gravity With calciuria present Low — Isosthenuria tap ▾
Kidneys unable to concentrate urine — tubular dysfunction confirmed.
Low specific gravity (approximately 1.008–1.012) reflects hypercalcemia-induced nephrogenic diabetes insipidus-like syndrome. Calciuria — calcium in the urine — is also present, providing direct evidence of calcium overflow from the bloodstream into the urinary tract and further confirming the diagnosis.
☀️ 25-Hydroxycholecalciferol Detectable in bile and kidney tissue Elevated tap ▾
Confirmatory finding — definitive when exposure history is uncertain.
High levels of 25-hydroxycholecalciferol and its active metabolite in bile and kidney tissue confirm the diagnosis definitively. This test is particularly valuable when the owner is unsure of the exposure source. Note: results are not always immediately available — clinical diagnosis and treatment should not be delayed while waiting for this result.

Other Differentials

Click each condition to reveal the key differentiating feature.

Key Differentiator
Caused by parathyroid hormone-related peptide (PTHrP) secreted by tumors. PTHrP mimics PTH activity but is detected separately on assay panels.

How to distinguish: PTHrP is elevated (not PTH itself). Look for evidence of underlying neoplasia — lymphadenopathy, splenomegaly, or abnormal cytology. No history of toxin exposure. Thoracic and abdominal imaging is essential in any hypercalcemic dog where toxin exposure cannot be confirmed.
Key Differentiator
Caused by a functional parathyroid adenoma or hyperplasia. The gland fails to suppress PTH despite high serum calcium — PTH is inappropriately elevated.

How to distinguish: PTH is elevated or inappropriately normal despite hypercalcemia — the opposite of cholecalciferol toxicosis, where PTH is suppressed. Cervical ultrasound may identify a parathyroid mass. No toxin exposure history.
Key Differentiator
Seen particularly in young Labrador Retrievers — the same breed as Buddy. Mechanism is not fully understood; possibly related to PTHrP or dietary influences.

How to distinguish: Diagnosis of exclusion — rule out toxin exposure, neoplasia, and primary hyperparathyroidism first. PTH and PTHrP may be equivocal. Responds to dietary calcium restriction. Buddy's breed makes this a genuine differential alongside the toxicosis history.
Key Differentiator
Both toxicoses cause renal damage and electrolyte disturbances, but the mechanisms differ. Ethylene glycol forms calcium oxalate crystals that deposit in renal tubules — a pathognomonic finding.

Kidney tissue analysis:
• Kidney Ca: >8,000 ppm (vs. 2,000–3,000 with cholecalciferol)
• Ca:P ratio in kidney: >2.5 (vs. 0.4–0.7 with cholecalciferol)
• Oxalate crystals on urine microscopy — pathognomonic for EG
• History of access to antifreeze
Bottom line: In any hypercalcemic dog or cat, the combination of toxin exposure history + suppressed PTH + phosphorus rising before calcium makes cholecalciferol toxicosis your working diagnosis until tissue analysis or a definitive alternate cause proves otherwise.
