⚠ Critical Values Present
📈 Serum Phosphorus Rises before calcium — key early marker 9.1 mg/dL ↑ tap ▾
Rises first — earliest detectable abnormality.
The critical threshold is >8 mg/dL. In cholecalciferol toxicosis, phosphorus elevation precedes the calcium rise — this temporal sequence helps distinguish this toxicosis from other causes of hypercalcemia. Monitor phosphorus closely in any suspected exposure case.
🔴 Total Calcium Normal canine range: 9–11 mg/dL 15.2 mg/dL ↑↑ tap ▾
Critically elevated — above the 13 mg/dL diagnostic threshold.
A total calcium above 13 mg/dL confirms significant hypercalcemia. Calculate: Ca × P = 15.2 × 9.1 = 138 mg/L — well above the 70 mg/L mineralization threshold.
🔬 Parathyroid Hormone (PTH) Critical differentiating finding Low / Suppressed tap ▾
Suppressed PTH with high calcium — a key differentiator.
The parathyroid gland suppresses PTH in response to high calcium. This distinguishes cholecalciferol poisoning from primary hyperparathyroidism where PTH would be inappropriately elevated despite high calcium.
🫘 BUN & Creatinine Renal function markers Both Elevated tap ▾
Azotemia confirms acute kidney injury (AKI).
Calcium deposits in renal tubules impair glomerular filtration and tubular function. Serial monitoring every 12–24 hours is essential. IV fluid diuresis is urgently needed to protect remaining renal function and promote calcium excretion.
💧 Urine Specific Gravity With calciuria present Low — Isosthenuria tap ▾
Kidneys unable to concentrate urine — tubular dysfunction confirmed.
Low specific gravity (~1.008–1.012) reflects hypercalcemia-induced nephrogenic diabetes insipidus-like syndrome. Calciuria — calcium in the urine — further confirms the diagnosis.
☀️ 25-Hydroxycholecalciferol Detectable in bile and kidney tissue Elevated tap ▾
Confirmatory finding — definitive when exposure history is uncertain.
High levels in bile and kidney tissue confirm the diagnosis. Not always immediately available — clinical diagnosis and treatment should not be delayed while waiting for this result.

Other Differentials

Key Differentiator
Caused by PTHrP secreted by tumors. PTHrP mimics PTH activity but is detected separately on assay panels.

How to distinguish: PTHrP is elevated (not PTH itself). Look for lymphadenopathy, splenomegaly, or abnormal cytology. No history of toxin exposure.
Key Differentiator
The gland fails to suppress PTH despite high serum calcium — PTH is inappropriately elevated.

How to distinguish: PTH is elevated or inappropriately normal despite hypercalcemia — the opposite of cholecalciferol toxicosis. Cervical ultrasound may identify a parathyroid mass. No toxin exposure history.
Key Differentiator
Seen particularly in young Labrador Retrievers — Buddy's breed.

How to distinguish: Diagnosis of exclusion — rule out toxin exposure, neoplasia, and primary hyperparathyroidism first. Buddy's breed makes this a genuine differential alongside the toxicosis history.
Key Differentiator
Ethylene glycol forms calcium oxalate crystals in renal tubules — pathognomonic.

• Kidney Ca: >8,000 ppm (vs. 2,000–3,000 with cholecalciferol)
• Ca:P ratio in kidney: >2.5 (vs. 0.4–0.7)
• Oxalate crystals on urine microscopy — pathognomonic
• History of access to antifreeze
Bottom line: The combination of toxin exposure history + suppressed PTH + phosphorus rising before calcium makes cholecalciferol toxicosis your working diagnosis until tissue analysis or a definitive alternate cause proves otherwise.
